# Running plateau and exertional-breathlessness follow-up

## Session lesson

When a runner reports that very easy running is comfortable but a modest pace increase causes rapid breathlessness and a sharp loss of endurance after roughly 1–2 km, do not reduce the explanation to either "overtraining" or "you simply need to run slower." If the athlete has already reported normal iron/blood tests and a strong exercise test, acknowledge that evidence and stop re-prescribing those investigations as the primary explanation. Keep training advice conditional and consider sport-specific follow-up such as exercise-induced bronchoconstriction or breathing-pattern dysfunction if the pattern persists, while respecting prior medical workup.

## Coaching interpretation

A useful training hypothesis is a narrow current intensity band: easy pace is sustainable, but the athlete crosses the ventilatory/aerobic threshold quickly when pace rises. Slower easy running can build aerobic durability, but a development plan should retain one controlled quality stimulus rather than making every session slower. Use effort and repeatability rather than rigid pace; progress one variable at a time (duration, total work, or pace).

Suggested provisional structure for a runner seeking improved speed/fitness:
- 2 genuinely easy conversational runs
- 1 controlled quality session, with a bailout option and no all-out finish
- an optional gradual easy long run, only when recovery is good
- strength and rest placed so hard sessions are separated

Do not promise improvement after a few sessions; assess the block after roughly 6–8 consistent weeks, while escalating medical review sooner if symptoms worsen or include red flags.

## Conversation continuity

Before asking the athlete to restate their goal, inspect the current conversation and available athlete context. If the goal is not visible in the current context, say that plainly and ask for a one-line reminder; do not pretend the goal is known and do not answer with a generic "if your goal is..." plan. When the athlete corrects the coach with "you know my goal," acknowledge the continuity failure directly and repair it before prescribing.
