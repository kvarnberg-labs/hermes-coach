# Running plateau, exertional breathlessness and long-term decline

Load when easy running is fine but slightly faster running causes rapid
breathlessness, when breathlessness persists despite a genuinely easy block, or
when an athlete reports a multi-year decline in easy pace.

## Red flags — refer, do not coach through

Breathlessness at rest, chest pain, fainting or near-fainting, cyanosis, or
marked palpitations → urgent medical care. Persistent exertional
breathlessness → recommend medical follow-up (ask about spirometry /
bronchodilator testing where appropriate). See
`coach-brain/coaching-safety.yaml → medical_referral` and
`coach-brain/persistent-performance-decline.yaml`.

## Exertional breathlessness

- First name the symptom precisely: heavy legs, unusual HR response, muscular
  fatigue, energy depletion, or breathlessness out of proportion to effort.
  Exercise-only symptoms are not automatically overtraining.
- Respect prior workup: normal blood/iron tests and a strong exercise test make
  anaemia, iron deficiency and thyroid disease less likely. Acknowledge them;
  do not keep re-proposing them as the explanation. They do not exclude
  exercise-induced bronchoconstriction, breathing-pattern dysfunction,
  post-viral/autonomic issues, altered running economy or pacing mismatch.
- If the athlete already did "a few easy days", do not prescribe more of the
  same; move to differential assessment and medical follow-up.

## Training hypothesis and structure

A narrow intensity band: easy pace is sustainable, but the athlete crosses the
ventilatory threshold quickly when pace rises. Keep one controlled quality
stimulus rather than slowing everything:

- 2 genuinely easy conversational runs
- 1 controlled quality session with a bail-out option, no all-out finish
- optional easy long run, only when recovery is good
- strength and rest placed so hard sessions are separated

Progress one variable at a time by effort and repeatability, not rigid pace.
Review the block after ~6–8 consistent weeks (planned vs actual, breathing,
RPE); escalate sooner if symptoms worsen.

## Multi-year decline in easy pace

Answer the causal question directly. A decline like 5:00 → 6:00/km at unchanged
volume is not explained by age alone — say so. Rank hypotheses:

1. Running-specific economy, threshold, leg durability, injury/biomechanics,
   body-composition change.
2. Chronic under-recovery or low energy availability (RED-S).
3. Post-viral/autonomic or other medical causes not seen in routine bloods.
4. Measurement/context: surface, terrain, heat, watch, pacing.

A normal cycling work ECG that stopped on leg fatigue below predicted max HR is
reassuring for major cardiac problems but says nothing about running economy or
threshold. Recent CTL/ATL/TSB — especially cycling-dominated or short series —
cannot explain a decline that began years earlier; present current fatigue as a
possible amplifier only. Use the profile instead of asking for age/weight,
separate Run from Ride, and check the date range before claiming a trend. For a
large unexplained decline, suggest a sports-medicine workup (running
CPET/lactate threshold, running economy, biomechanics/strength, energy
availability) without diagnosing.

## Continuity

Check the conversation and saved memory for the athlete's goal before asking.
If it is not available, say so and ask for a one-line reminder — no generic
"if your goal is…" plans. If the athlete says "you know my goal", acknowledge
the continuity failure and repair it before prescribing.
